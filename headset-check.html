The ear cup lights while its channel is playing, and goes dark when the tone stops. Mark a side Silent and it turns red and stays that way, because a side that never played is a different problem from one that played in the wrong ear.

The tone check chooses what the channel check plays. Pick a tone and a sound, then use Left, Right or Both above to hear it — choosing on its own makes no sound. The string note is generated in the browser rather than recorded, so it is the same note every time and nothing is downloaded.

Polarity

Plays the same tone in both channels, then again with one of them inverted. The two should sound different: solid and centred the first time, thin and spread out the second. If they sound the same, one driver is wired backwards — and the left and right test cannot see that, because a reversed driver still arrives in the right ear.

Frequency sweep

Sweeps a tone from 20 Hz up to 1 kHz over eight seconds, in both channels at once. Listen for where the sound stops being there: headphones roll off at the bottom, and the low notes are the first thing you stop hearing. Your own hearing is in the measurement too, so a tone you cannot hear is not proof of a fault.

Turn the volume down first. The sweep ends at 1 kHz, which is where a test tone starts to bite.

Troubleshooting

Headphones not behaving?

Seven faults account for almost every headphone complaint. Each one below says what causes it, what this page can settle for you, and what to try first — in the order you should try it.

Why is the sound in the wrong ear?

Your two channels have crossed. The usual cause is the headphones being on the wrong way round — look for the L and R marks inside the earcups — and the next most common is a cable, adapter or splitter with the wiring reversed. A repair or a replacement cable can leave a pair wired backwards without anything looking wrong.

Play Left, then Right. If each tone arrives in the opposite ear, that is what has happened.

Why does only one side play?

Four things to check, in this order, and stop as soon as the sound comes back. First, the left and right balance slider in your system or device sound settings: a slider nudged one way is the most common cause of headphones that play in one ear only, and it costs nothing to check. Second, the plug — push it fully home, and clean the tip if it is dirty or oxidised. Third, the output device: make sure the headphones are the selected output and the tab is not muted. Fourth, the cable, above the split or at the plug, where it gets bent.

Then swap something to find out whose fault it is. Try another pair of headphones on the same device, and the same headphones on another device. If the second pair also plays on one side, the fault is in the device or its settings. If the same headphones fail everywhere, the fault is in the headphones.

Why is one side quieter than the other?

Start with the balance slider, because it is the cause far more often than the hardware. Every phone and computer has one, and on a phone it usually lives under Accessibility rather than Sound, which is why it can go unnoticed for years. A single channel turned down a little is enough to make one side sound quieter, and it does the same to every app on the device.

If the balance is centred and one side is still quieter, put the headphones on the other way round and listen again. If the quiet side moves with the headphones, they are the cause. If it stays in the same ear, the cause is the fit of the earbud in that ear or your hearing, not the equipment. This page cannot measure the difference in level — only you can hear it — so the tone check is there to give you something steady to compare the two sides against.

Why do I hear the same sound in both ears?

The signal reaching the headphones has been reduced to a single channel, so there is nothing left to tell left from right. A mono adapter, a three-pole plug in a four-pole socket, and a mono audio setting in the operating system will each do it.

The channel check settles it in one press. Play Left: if the tone sounds identical in both ears, the signal is mono. A mono signal collapses every stereo cue — where a voice sits in a film, which side a car is coming from — into the middle of your head.

Why is there no sound at all?

The tone is generated in your browser, so if you can hear nothing at all, the sound never left the page. Check that the tab is not muted, that the system volume is up, and that the headphones are the selected output device rather than the built-in speakers. Browsers also throttle audio in a background tab, so keep the page in front of you.

On a Bluetooth headset the connection itself is the usual culprit. Remove the device from the Bluetooth menu and pair it again, and make sure it is not connected to something else at the same time.

Why does one side crackle, buzz or distort?

A driver that has started to fail usually breaks up at one frequency rather than everywhere, so play the three tones in turn and listen for the pitch that does not survive. If the buzz is there on every tone, it is more likely the operating system's own processing — spatial audio, a mono audio setting, a virtual surround mode — than the headphones. Turning those off is worth trying before you write the pair off.

Why does a Bluetooth headset keep dropping a side?

Almost always the link rather than the headphones. An earbud that is not seated in the case, or not charged, will refuse to play while the other carries on, and a pair can end up half-paired to two devices at once. Put both buds back in the case, close it, pair again, and check that nothing else is connected. If a wired pair behaves the same way on the same machine, the fault is in that machine's audio settings rather than in the Bluetooth.

What this page cannot tell you

It cannot measure anything about your headphones. A browser can send a tone down a channel, but it cannot hear what arrives, so the channel check, the balance and the frequency response all come down to what you tell it. What it does give you is a quiet way to make the comparison: a tone in one channel at a time, a drawing that shows which side is playing, and a record of what you marked. A measurement rather than a comparison is a different kind of tool.

FAQ

Headphone test questions

How do I test if my headphones are working?

Put the headphones on, turn the volume down, then press Left. You should hear the tone in your left ear only; press Right and it should move to the right ear. Press Both and the sound should sit in the middle of your head, at the same level on both sides. Then mark each ear and the page keeps your answers and tells you what that combination of marks means. It is a headphone test you can run in ten seconds, and nothing has to be installed.

How do I check whether my left and right are swapped?

Play Left and then Right, and mark what you heard on each press. If the tone arrived in the opposite ear each time, your channels are crossed: the headphones are on the wrong way round, or a cable, adapter or splitter has the wiring reversed. If both presses sounded in both ears, the signal is mono instead. The page cannot tell those two apart for you — it is a listening test, and the answer is what you report back to it.

Why is one ear quieter than the other?

Check the left and right balance slider in your device's sound settings first. On a phone it is usually under Accessibility rather than Sound. If the balance is centred and one side is still quieter, put the headphones on the other way round: if the quiet side follows the headphones, they are the cause; if it stays in the same ear, it is the fit or your hearing. The tone check gives you three fixed pitches to compare the two sides against.

Why does only one side of my headphones play?

The balance slider, a plug that is not fully in, an output device that is not your headphones, or a broken cable. Work down that list in order — the slider is the most common and the least obvious of the four. If you have already swapped to another pair and the new one also plays on one side, the fault is in the device or its settings rather than in the headphones.

What does it mean if I hear the same sound in both ears?

It means the signal is mono. A mono adapter, a three-pole plug in a four-pole socket and a mono audio setting in the operating system will each do it. A mono signal cannot tell left from right, so every stereo cue — where a voice sits in a film, which side a car is coming from — collapses into the middle. Press Left in the channel check: if the tone sounds identical in both ears, that is what has happened.

Can this test measure my headphone frequency response or sound quality?

No, and it does not pretend to. A browser can generate a tone; it cannot hear what comes back. The tone check gives you three fixed pitches to listen to, which is enough to spot a driver that has failed or a cable that is breaking up, and to hear the roll-off at the bottom of the range that most headphones have. Measuring a frequency response properly needs a reference signal played back and captured, and capturing it needs a microphone — a different kind of tool.

Does it work with earbuds and Bluetooth headphones?

Yes, with one caveat. The tone is generated in the browser and sent through whatever the operating system has chosen as the output, so Bluetooth is included — and a Bluetooth pair has faults of its own, such as an earbud not seated in the case or a device paired to two things at once. The caveat is that Bluetooth codecs compress the signal, so do not read the timbre of a wireless pair as a fault.

Why is a tone missing at one end of the range?

Two reasons, and both are ordinary. Headphones roll off at the bottom: the 100 Hz tone is the one most pairs play quietest, because the ear hears it less well and because most headphones give up their deepest bass first. And the roll-off is not even between sides, so a note that sounds full in one ear can sound hollow in the other if the two drivers are not matched. If a tone is missing rather than quiet, and the same happens on another device, then a driver has failed.

Can this test my headset microphone?

No. This page covers the headphone half of a headset. The microphone half can be measured rather than guessed at, because the browser can read the signal it asks for and report the processing it was given — but that is a separate test, and it is not on this page.
