The ear cup lights while its channel is playing, and goes dark when the tone stops. Mark a side Silent and it turns red and stays that way, because a side that never played is a different problem from one that played in the wrong ear.

The tone check chooses what the channel check plays. Pick a tone and a sound, then use Left, Right or Both above to hear it — choosing on its own makes no sound. The string note is generated in the browser rather than recorded, so it is the same note every time and nothing is downloaded.

Polarity

Plays the same tone in both channels, then again with the right channel inverted, then the first version again. With a correctly wired pair the first version sits in the middle of your head and the second spreads out to both sides. If the second version is the one that feels centred, one driver is wired backwards — and the left and right test cannot see that.

Frequency sweep

Sweeps a tone from 20 Hz up to 1 kHz over eight seconds, in both channels at once. Listen for where the sound stops being there: headphones roll off at the bottom, and the low notes are the first thing you stop hearing. Your own hearing is in the measurement too, so a tone you cannot hear is not proof of a fault.

Turn the volume down first. The sweep ends at 1 kHz, which is where a test tone starts to bite.

Troubleshooting

Headphones not behaving?

Eight faults account for almost every headphone complaint. Each one below says what causes it and what to try first. The headphone troubleshooting guide has the long version of all of them, with the settings to check on each platform.

Why is the sound in the wrong ear?

Your two channels have crossed. The usual cause is the headphones being on the wrong way round — look for the L and R marks inside the earcups. Play Left, then Right: if each tone arrives in the opposite ear, that is what has happened. The full fault

Why does one ear sound thin, hollow or wider than the other?

One driver is playing with its phase flipped, so the two sides partly cancel instead of summing. It is a wiring fault rather than a driver fault, which is why the channel check passes and the fault survives it. The polarity check finds it. The full fault

Why does only one side play?

Four things to check, in order: the balance slider in your sound settings, the plug, the output device, and the cable. Then swap to find out whose fault it is — another pair on this machine, and this pair on another machine. The full fault

Why is one side quieter than the other?

Start with the balance slider, because it is the cause far more often than the hardware. If the balance is centred, put the headphones on the other way round: if the quiet side moves with the headphones, they are the cause. The full fault

Why do I hear the same sound in both ears?

The signal has been reduced to a single channel. A mono adapter, a three-pole plug in a four-pole socket, or mono audio switched on in the operating system will each do it. Play Left: if the tone sounds identical in both ears, that is what has happened. The full fault

Why is there no sound at all?

The sound never left the page. Check that the tab is not muted, that the volume is up, and that the headphones are the selected output device. Browsers throttle audio in a background tab, so keep the page in front of you. The full fault

Why does one side crackle, buzz or distort?

A driver that is failing usually breaks up at one frequency rather than everywhere, so play the three tones in turn and listen for the pitch that does not survive. If the buzz is on every tone, it is more likely the operating system's spatial processing than the headphones. The full fault

Why does a Bluetooth headset keep dropping a side?

Almost always the link rather than the headphones: an earbud not seated in the case, a low charge, or a second device competing for the connection. Put both buds back in the case, close it, and pair again. The full fault

What this page cannot tell you

FAQ

Headphone test questions

The short answers. The headphone troubleshooting guide has the long ones.

How do I test if my headphones are working?

Put them on, turn the volume down to about a third, then press Left, Right and Both in turn. The left tone should arrive in your left ear only, the right tone in your right ear only, and Both should sit in the middle of your head. Mark each side and the page tells you what your answers mean. Nothing has to be installed.

How do I check whether my left and right are swapped?

Play Left and then Right, and mark what you heard. If each tone arrived in the opposite ear, your channels are crossed: the headphones are on the wrong way round, or a cable or adapter has the wiring reversed.

Why is one ear quieter than the other?

Check the left and right balance slider in your device's sound settings first — on a phone it is usually under Accessibility rather than Sound. If the balance is centred, put the headphones on the other way round: if the quiet side follows the headphones, they are the cause; if it stays in the same ear, it is the fit or your hearing.

Why does only one side of my headphones play?

The balance slider, a plug that is not fully in, an output device that is not your headphones, or a broken cable. Work down that list in order — the slider is the most common and the least obvious of the four.

What does it mean if I hear the same sound in both ears?

It means the signal is mono. A mono adapter, a three-pole plug in a four-pole socket, and a mono audio setting in the operating system will each do it. Press Left in the channel check: if the tone sounds identical in both ears, that is what has happened.

How do I check whether one of my headphone drivers is wired backwards?

Use the polarity check in the advanced diagnostics. It plays a tone in both channels, then again with one channel inverted, and asks which version was centred. The first version centred means the wiring is correct; the second version centred means one driver is arriving with its phase flipped.

Why does one ear sound thin, hollow or wider than the other?

Because the two drivers are not summing: one side is playing with its phase flipped and partly cancels the other. It is a wiring fault rather than a failed driver, so the tone still reaches the correct ear and the channel check passes.

Can this test measure my headphone frequency response or sound quality?

No, and it does not pretend to. A browser can generate a tone; it cannot hear what comes back. Measuring a frequency response properly needs a reference signal played back and captured, and capturing it needs a microphone — a different kind of tool.

Does it work with earbuds and Bluetooth headphones?

Yes. The tone is generated in the browser and sent through whatever the operating system has chosen as the output, so Bluetooth is included. The one caveat is that Bluetooth codecs compress the signal, so do not read the timbre of a wireless pair as a fault.

Why is a tone missing at one end of the range?

Usually ordinary. Headphones roll off at the bottom, and the 100 Hz tone is the one most pairs play quietest, because the ear hears it less well and most headphones give up their deepest bass first. If a tone is missing rather than quiet, and the same happens on another device, a driver has failed.

Why does my Bluetooth headset keep dropping one side?

Almost always the link rather than the headphones: an earbud not seated in the case, a low charge, or a second device competing for the connection. Put both buds back in the case, close the lid, and pair the device again.

Can this test my headset microphone?

No. This page covers the headphone half of a headset. The microphone half can be measured rather than guessed at, but that is a separate test and it is not here yet.
