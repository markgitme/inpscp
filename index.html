Coverage

What these tests actually check

Every measurement below maps to a specific, named behaviour in your device. Nothing here is a generic "click anywhere" widget that gives you a meaningless score.

✓ Pointer precision

Small targets appear at random positions and you click them one at a time. InputScope records how often you hit the centre, how far off your misses land, and your longest clean run. A steady decline in accuracy across a session usually means a dirty or worn sensor, not a bad mouse.

✓ Polling rate

The raw pointer event stream is sampled for one second while you move the mouse. Where the browser exposes raw sampling you get a true hardware figure — 125 Hz on a budget office mouse, 500 Hz on a mid-range one, 1000 Hz on a gaming sensor. The result always states which method produced the number.

✓ Double-click timing

Your operating system has a double-click window, usually 500 ms. If your mouse is slow enough that the two clicks fall outside that window, the system treats them as two single clicks and selects nothing. This test measures the actual interval and tells you whether your setting needs raising.

✓ Scroll and encoder wear

Scroll wheels are mechanical encoders, and they wear out long before the rest of the mouse. Missing or duplicated notches are the classic symptom. The scroll test counts notches in each direction and reports the pixel delta per notch, which makes skipping obvious.

✓ Key rollover depth

Pressing two keys at once only registers both if the keyboard has anti-ghosting hardware. InputScope holds Shift, Ctrl, Alt and a letter simultaneously and reports how many the keyboard actually delivered. Three or more is the bar most gaming keyboards clear; two is the bare minimum.

✓ Chattering and double-fire

A worn key switch starts making contact twice per press. On the live key map, a switch that chatters shows extra keydown events for a single physical press. The key count and the event log make that visible in a way that simply looking at the screen does not.

Common problems

Looking for a specific fault?

These are the questions people search for most often, and every one of them is answered in full on the relevant tool page.

Why is my mouse not double-clicking?

Usually a double-click window that is too tight, occasionally a worn switch. The mouse page has the fix for each, and a test that measures your actual interval.

Why does my mouse keep clicking on its own?

A switch that is making contact at rest, or a wireless link reporting a stale button state. The click tester tells you which.

Why is my scroll wheel jittering or skipping?

A worn scroll encoder, which is usually the first part of a mouse to fail. How to clean it, and how to tell it apart from a bad surface.

Why are some keys on my keyboard not working?

Liquid, wear or debris under the cap. The key tester shows you whether a key is dead or merely unreliable.

Why does my keyboard type the wrong character?

Almost never a broken keyboard — nearly always a layout mismatch between the hardware and the operating system.

Why is my keyboard ghosting?

The keyboard cannot tell which of several keys closed its circuit. The rollover test reports exactly how many it handles at once.
Why this exists

Peripherals fail quietly

Hardware gets replaced on a schedule nobody sets. The mouse that was fine last year now needs two attempts to open a menu, or the left edge of a window catches a stray pixel movement. Nobody can quite say when it started, so the problem gets attributed to the operating system, the application, or "the computer being slow".

InputScope exists to make that vague feeling measurable. The value is not in the score — it is in the comparison. Run the mouse test on a surface you trust, then run it again on a different one, or again in three months, and you have a baseline. When something genuinely changes, you will know, and you will know which part changed.

There is no account, no paywall and no download. The tools are deliberately small, and the site is deliberately independent — no affiliate links disguised as reviews, no sponsored "best mice" listicles, and no advertising on the diagnostic pages themselves. If the site ever carries ads, they will sit on the informational pages only.

What InputScope will not tell you

A web page cannot read your operating system's pointer-speed slider, your mouse's true sensor DPI, or the exact hardware part number. It measures what reaches the browser: the timing and geometry of the input events themselves.

Every result that is an estimate is labelled as an estimate. That is the whole point of a diagnostic tool.

Running on a locked-down machine?

The tests need a mouse or keyboard attached and a browser that supports Pointer Events and Keyboard Events. If you are on a shared or managed device, a wired input device gives the most consistent readings, because wireless links add their own timing jitter.

A note on laptop trackpads

Most of these results are calibrated for a physical mouse. A trackpad's pointer is driven by a finger, so precision, hover and acceleration results will look very different — useful for a rough comparison, not for judging the hardware.

FAQ

Questions people ask about input tests

Do these tests upload my data?

No. Every test measures raw pointer and keyboard events inside your browser tab using the standard Pointer Events and Keyboard Events APIs. There is no server call, no telemetry, and no account to create. Results stay in memory until you close the tab. The one thing on this site that does send anything is the contact form, and it only sends what you choose to type into it — the privacy policy has the details.

Can I really measure my mouse's true DPI in a browser?

Not exactly, and InputScope does not pretend otherwise. A web page cannot read your operating system's pointer speed setting or the hardware sensor resolution. What it can do is measure the visible consequences: how far the cursor travels for a given hand movement, how consistent that distance is, how tightly you can hit a small target, and how fast raw input samples arrive. Those are the numbers the tests report.

Why did my typing speed look different from the number in my old speed test?

Most online speed tests include autocorrect, dictionary suggestions, and text completion, which inflate the result. InputScope measures raw keystrokes against a fixed sample and counts only characters that match, so the number is usually lower and more honest. It also reports accuracy alongside words per minute.

Is a low score a sign my device is broken?

Usually not. Scores vary a lot with the surface you use, the sensitivity setting, your hand size, and how you sit. Treat the number as a baseline to compare against. The useful signal is a sudden change: a sharp drop in pointer accuracy or a slow double-click interval usually points to a worn switch, a dirty sensor, or a failing scroll encoder rather than a bad day.

Does the polling rate test work everywhere?

Only in browsers that expose the raw pointer update event, which today means Chromium-based browsers. Elsewhere InputScope falls back to counting standard pointer move events, which is a lower bound rather than a true hardware rate. The result panel labels which method was used so the number is never mistaken for more than it is.

Should I test with a mousepad or a bare desk?

Test in the condition you actually use, then test it a second time on a clean, consistent surface. Optical sensors rely on a texture to track movement, and a bare polished desk or a slick glass surface produces different readings from a fabric mousepad. If the two results diverge sharply, the surface is the variable, not the mouse.

Can I use these on a trackpad or a laptop keyboard?

You can, but read the results with that in mind. A trackpad is finger-driven, so precision and acceleration figures will be far lower than a mouse. A laptop keyboard is usually a low-profile membrane or scissor mechanism with a noticeably different key travel, and many of them are deliberately limited to two-key rollover, which the rollover test will show you clearly.

How do I test if my mouse is working?

Open the Mouse Check page and use the click tester at the top. Press every button in turn — left, right, the scroll wheel, and the thumb buttons if you have them — and the interactive mouse diagram shows which one registered. If everything lights up and the scroll counter climbs, the mouse is working. If one button never lights up, that button is the fault.

How do I test if my keyboard is working?

Open the Keyboard Check page and use the key tester. Every key you press lights up its matching cap on the live map, so a dead key, a key that stays stuck down and a key that fires twice are all obvious on the first walk through the keyboard.

Will this site have ads?

Possibly, eventually, and only on the informational pages — the explanations, guides and reference sections. The diagnostic tools themselves stay ad-free, because a layout shift caused by an ad injecting into a measurement surface would corrupt the results. Any advertising partner will be disclosed in the footer and the privacy policy.
