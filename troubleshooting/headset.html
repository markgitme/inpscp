What this page can and cannot tell you

Start with the honest limit, because it shapes everything else. A browser can send a tone down a channel. It cannot hear what arrives. That single fact decides which faults a web page can settle and which ones it can only help you investigate.

The Headset Check page generates every sound itself, in your browser, and it can do three useful things. It can send a tone to the left channel only, or the right channel only, or both. It can put the same tone on one channel with its phase flipped, which is how a reversed driver is found. And it can sweep a pitch across the range so you can hear where your pair gives up.

What it cannot do is measure. It has no microphone, so it cannot capture what came out of the headphone and compare it against what went in. Every judgement about your headphones is therefore one you make by listening and then reporting back. The page records what you report and turns it into a verdict, and the verdict is only ever as good as the answer behind it. That is a different kind of tool from the mouse test or the keyboard test, which read the device directly and compare it against a threshold.

So the honest way to use it is as a comparison instrument rather than a measurement. It gives you a steady tone, a clean way to isolate one channel, and a record of what you marked. When two readings disagree — one side silent on one machine, fine on another — the fault is in the chain, and this page helps you find which part of it.

One more thing worth knowing before you start. A test tone is not music. A sine wave carries far more energy at the same volume setting than a track does, because music spends most of its time at low levels and a tone does not. Start with the system volume at about a third, keep the page in front of you rather than in a background tab, and raise it only once you know what you are listening to.

Why is the sound in the wrong ear?

Your two channels have crossed. The most common cause is not a fault at all: the headphones are on the wrong way round. Look inside the headband or on the inner face of each earcup for an L and an R. On many pairs the letter is on the side that faces your head, so a pair put on back to front still plays, and still sounds normal in music, because most music does not put much in one channel alone.

The second most common cause is a cable, adapter or splitter with the wiring reversed. A replacement cable fitted by a repair shop can be wired correctly for the microphone and incorrectly for the channels. A detachable cable is the easiest thing to get wrong, because the two ends are not interchangeable: the side with the microphone goes to the headset, and the other side goes to the device. On many pairs they will physically fit either way.

A third cause, and the one people check last, is a headphone jack that is wired as a headset rather than as headphones. A four-pole jack carries a microphone and a ground shared between the two channels. A three-pole plug in a four-pole socket can leave one channel grounded, and the result is a headphone that plays in one ear only.

There is one more possibility that is not a fault in the headphones at all: the operating system or the application is sending the channels the wrong way round. It is rare, but it does happen with a badly configured surround or virtual surround mode, and with some audio routing software.

The test that settles it

Play Left, then Right, and mark what you heard on each press. If each tone arrives in the opposite ear, the channels are crossed. If a tone arrives in both ears, that is a different fault — see why you hear the same sound in both ears. Run the channel check

Look for the letters

Inside the headband, on the inner face of each earcup, or printed on the cable near the plug. If the L and R are on the outside, the pair goes on the other way round from what the letters suggest.

Turn the headphones round

Take them off and put them on the other way round, then play Left again. If the tone now arrives in the left ear, that was the whole fault.

Check the detachable cable

The end with the microphone and volume control goes to the headset. The plain end goes to the device. They are not interchangeable on most pairs, and they will often fit anyway.

Remove everything in the chain

Plug the headphones directly into the device. Extension cables, splitters, monitor hubs and inline remotes each add a place for the wiring to be wrong. Test with the remote disconnected.

Try another device

The same headphones on a phone and a different pair on the same machine. If the swap follows the headphones, the fault is in the headphones; if it stays with the machine, the fault is in the machine.

Why does only one side play?

Four things to check, in this order, and stop as soon as the sound comes back.

First, the balance slider. It is the most common cause of headphones that play in one ear only, it costs nothing to check, and it is the one people miss. On a computer it is a slider labelled balance, somewhere in the sound settings. Where exactly depends on the system, and the steps are below.

Second, the plug. Push it fully home until it clicks, and look at the tip. A plug that is half in may connect the sleeve and one channel only. Clean the tip if it is dirty or oxidised, and look inside the socket for lint, which collects at the bottom and holds the plug up.

Third, the output device. Make sure the headphones are the selected output, that the tab is not muted, and that the volume is not on a different slider. On a machine with several outputs — built-in speakers, a monitor, a headset — the default can change when a device is plugged in or removed.

Fourth, the cable. The failure point is above the split or at the plug, where the cable gets bent. A wire inside can break completely, or part way, and a half-broken wire is the more annoying version because it cuts in and out as you move it.

Then find out whose fault it is. Try another pair of headphones on the same machine, and the same headphones on another machine. If the second pair also plays on one side, the fault is in the device or its settings. If the same headphones fail everywhere, the fault is in the headphones.

The test that settles it

Play Left and then Right, and mark each side. A side that is silent on both presses is the side to work on. Then play a tone and flex the cable gently where it leaves the plug, and near the split: if the sound cuts in and out as you move it, the fault is in the cable. Run the channel check

Centre the balance slider

On Windows 11: Settings → System → Sound, then scroll to the bottom of the Output section and choose More sound settings. In the Sound dialog that opens, click your headphones on the Playback tab, then Properties, then the Levels tab, then Balance. Older versions of Windows reach the same dialog through Control Panel → Sound → Playback. If the Levels tab or the Balance button is not there, the device or its driver does not offer one — some Bluetooth and USB headphones control their own balance and hide it.

On macOS: System Settings → Sound → Output, click your headphones in the device list, and drag the Balance slider back to the centre. macOS also has a long-standing bug that lets the balance drift to one side on its own, so if it keeps coming back, that is the bug rather than your headphones.

Seat the plug properly

Push until it clicks and the sleeve is flush. Look into the socket for lint. Wiggling the plug while a tone is playing tells you a great deal: if the sound changes as you move it, the fault is at the connection, not inside the headphone.

Check the output device and the mixer

On Windows, the Volume mixer has a per-application slider and a mute checkbox that are easy to leave in the wrong state. On macOS, the output device picker is in Sound → Output.

Flex the cable while it plays

Work along the cable in short sections, at the plug and at the split. If the tone drops out anywhere, that is the section to replace.

Swap both ways

Another pair on this machine, and this pair on another machine. This is the only test that separates the two, and it takes ten seconds.

Why is one side quieter than the other?

Start with the balance slider, because it is the cause far more often than the hardware. It is a setting nobody thinks to look at, and it does the same to every app on the device, so it can go unnoticed for years. A single channel turned down a little is enough to make one side sound quieter. If the balance is already centred and one side is still quieter, put the headphones on the other way round and listen again: if the quiet side moves with the headphones, they are the cause; if it stays in the same ear, the cause is the fit or your hearing, not the equipment.

If the balance is centred and one side is still quieter, the next test is the swap test: put the headphones on the other way round, or if they are earbuds, swap them between ears, and listen again. If the quiet side moves with the headphones, they are the cause. If it stays in the same ear, the cause is the fit or your hearing, not the equipment.

That distinction matters more than it looks, because the two causes have nothing in common. A headphone fault is fixed by a repair or a replacement. A fit or hearing cause is fixed by a different ear tip, a different size, or nothing at all.

And on a Mac, add one more cause to the list before you blame the hardware: macOS has carried a bug for more than twenty years that lets the balance drift towards one channel by itself. If the slider is not centred, or keeps moving after you centre it, that is the bug, and no amount of cleaning will fix it.

For earbuds, the fit does more than comfort. A tip that is too small, or not seated deeply, lets low frequencies and overall loudness leak out around the edges, and that reads as quieter rather than as a fault. For over-ear headphones, the same thing happens when the cups do not seal evenly against your head.

Where the fault really sits, when it is in the headphones, is usually the cable or the driver rather than the earcup. A worn spot in a cable can attenuate one channel without killing it outright, which is why the quiet side often fades in and out as you move.

And a word on when to stop. If the balance is centred, the swap test has traced the fault to the earcup, the cable is clean, and the gap is still there, the honest conclusion is a driver that has degraded on that side. It does not happen suddenly, it is a gradual decline, and it does not recover.

The test that settles it

Play the same tone on both sides and compare them, then do the swap test. The tone check gives you three fixed pitches to compare the two sides against, which is far easier than comparing two pieces of music. Run the tone check

Why do I hear the same sound in both ears?

The signal reaching the headphones has been reduced to a single channel, so there is nothing left to tell left from right. A mono adapter, a three-pole plug in a four-pole socket, and a mono audio setting in the operating system will each do it. The operating system setting is the one that catches people, because it applies to every app on the device and nothing about it looks wrong.

A mono signal cannot tell left from right, so every stereo cue collapses into the middle: where a voice sits in a film, which side a car is coming from, which side a gunshot came from in a game. Music is the least noticeable case, because most stereo music mixes are not strongly directional. Films and games are where it becomes obvious.

There is a second cause with the same symptom, and it is worth separating. Some single-driver Bluetooth speakers and some cheap earbuds genuinely have one driver, and on those the two channels are physically the same speaker. If your Left and Right presses sound identical because the hardware only has one driver, the test is telling you the truth and there is nothing to fix.

And a third: if you are testing with the speakers rather than headphones, each speaker's sound reaches both ears. That is why a left and right audio test needs headphones to mean anything.

The test that settles it

Play Left. If the tone sounds identical in both ears, the signal is mono. Then play Both: a correct pair sounds like one source in the middle of your head. Run the channel check

Turn mono audio off

On Windows 11 it is a switch on the Sound page itself: Settings → System → Sound, and Mono audio is under the Output section, just below the volume slider. On macOS it is System Settings → Accessibility → Audio, where the option is called Play stereo as mono. On a phone the control exists under Accessibility on an iPhone and under Sound on some Android phones, and on a great many Android phones it does not exist at all — in which case the mono signal is coming from something else, and the plug and the adapter are the next things to look at.

Remove mono adapters and splitters

A single-earphone adapter with one socket in and two out is a mono splitter. So is a Y-cable sold as a headphone splitter. A proper stereo splitter has a separate left and right cable and is labelled as one.

Count the pins

A three-pole plug carries two channels and a ground. A four-pole jack carries two channels, a microphone and a shared ground. A three-pole plug in a four-pole socket may ground one channel, which sounds like one side playing faintly rather than not at all.

Check for a mono source

Some content is genuinely mono, and some applications mix to mono deliberately. Test with a film trailer or a game, not with an album you know well.

Why does one ear sound thin, hollow or wider than the other?

This is the fault the channel test cannot see, and it is worth a section of its own. It happens when one driver is playing with its phase flipped, so the two sides partly cancel instead of summing. The sound spreads to the sides of your head rather than sitting in the middle, and it can feel thin or hollow, or as though the sound is coming from outside rather than inside.

It is a wiring fault, not a driver fault. The driver still works, and it still arrives in the correct ear, which is exactly why the channel check passes and the fault survives it. A repair, a replacement cable, or a plug pushed in upside down can all leave a pair like this.

Here is what makes it confusing. With a correctly wired pair, the two versions of the tone sound different — and with a reversed driver they also sound different, but the order is swapped. The inverted version arrives in phase and becomes the centred one. So the useful question is not whether the two sounded the same or different, but which one was centred.

And the answer is a thing you can feel rather than a thing you can measure. A tone sent to both channels with nothing distinguishing the two ears has no direction at all: it is heard inside your head, on the midline. The same tone with one channel inverted cancels in the middle and spreads out to both sides. You do not need to be able to hear frequencies accurately to tell those two apart.

The test that settles it

Open the advanced diagnostics on the Headset Check page and use the polarity check. It plays the tone in both channels, then again with the right channel inverted, then the first version again, and asks which version was centred. The first version centred means the wiring is correct. The second version centred means one driver is arriving with its phase flipped. Run the polarity check

Look at the cable and the connector

A reversed driver means a reversed wire somewhere in the chain. A detachable cable fitted to the wrong end, a repaired plug, or a jack wired as a headset rather than as headphones will all do it.

Why is there no sound at all?

If the tone is generated in your browser and you can hear nothing at all, the sound never left the page. That is useful news: the fault is in the output chain rather than in the headphones, and you have not lost a driver.

Check the tab first. Browsers mute individual tabs, and the little speaker icon in the address bar is easy to miss. Then check the system volume, and check that the headphones are the selected output device rather than the built-in speakers — a machine with a monitor and a headset can switch its default when one is plugged in or removed.

Keep the page in front of you. Browsers throttle audio in a background tab, so a tab you have switched away from may be playing quietly or not at all. If you have to check something else while the tone runs, come back to the tab.

On a Bluetooth headset, the connection itself is the usual culprit. The pair may be connected to something else, or half-paired to two devices at once. Remove it from the Bluetooth menu and pair it again.

One more cause that catches people out on a desktop: an audio application that has taken exclusive control of the output device. A DAW, a virtual audio device, a screen recorder with audio monitoring, or a conference app with a virtual microphone can hold the device and leave the browser unable to open an output stream. Closing the app is the fix.

Why does one side crackle, buzz or distort?

A driver that has started to fail usually breaks up at one frequency rather than everywhere, which is why the tone check is worth running through its three pitches in turn. Listen for the pitch that does not survive. A buzz that is there on every tone is more likely the operating system's own processing than the headphones.

That processing is worth taking seriously, because it is common and it is free to turn off. Spatial audio, mono audio, virtual surround modes, sound effects and equalisation presets all process the signal before it reaches the driver, and a virtual surround mode in particular will push the channels around. On Windows it is in the device properties, under Advanced settings; on Apple platforms it is in Accessibility → Audio & Visual.

On the hardware side, three causes cover most of it. A cable with a broken conductor produces crackle that comes and goes as you move it, and it usually has a location you can find by flexing the section by section. A driver with a loose or damaged voice coil produces distortion that grows with volume and with bass, because the cone is being asked to move further than it can. And a blocked earbud mesh produces a quieter, duller sound that people often report as reduced quality rather than as a fault.

Bluetooth adds its own: a compressed codec will make a wireless pair sound different from a wired one, and some of that difference is normal rather than a fault. Do not read the timbre of a wireless pair as damage.

The test that settles it

Play the three pitches in turn and note which one breaks up. If the buzz is present on all three, turn off the operating system's spatial and effects processing and try again. Run the tone check

Why does a Bluetooth headset keep dropping a side?

Almost always the link rather than the headphones. An earbud that is not seated in the case, or not charged, will refuse to play while the other carries on, and a pair can end up half-paired to two devices at once.

There are three separate Bluetooth faults that all present as one side dropping, and they are worth telling apart. The first is a bud that is not making contact in the case: the charging contacts are dirty, or the bud is not seated, so one side never charges and dies first. The second is a desynchronised pairing: the two buds are still paired with each other but the phone has lost one of them, and only one reconnects. The third is a device competing for the connection, usually a laptop that reconnects the headset every few seconds while the phone is also holding it.

Wireless is also where the battery fault shows up most clearly. A pair that reports one bud at 10% and the other at 60% will sound unbalanced before it sounds silent, and a fully charged pair is a surprisingly common fix.

And a last one that has nothing to do with the headphones: the phone's own battery saving will turn Bluetooth off, or a headset profile off, when it thinks you are not using it. On Android, that shows up as audio switching to the phone speaker mid-song.

The test that settles it

Why is a tone missing at one end of the range?

Two reasons, and both are ordinary. Headphones roll off at the bottom. The 100 Hz tone is the one most pairs play quietest, because the ear hears it less well and because most headphones give up their deepest bass first. Some of that is the headphone and some of it is your hearing, and the two are indistinguishable from inside.

And the roll-off is not even between sides. A note that sounds full in one ear can sound hollow in the other, because the two drivers are not matched exactly. A pair that is a little uneven at 100 Hz is normal; a pair that is a little uneven at 1 kHz is not.

Here is the part that matters for the sweep. Your own hearing is in the measurement. A tone you cannot hear is not proof of a fault. Adults lose the highest frequencies first, and the top of the range is often simply gone before the headphones are. So the sweep is a comparison against yourself as much as against the headphones, and the honest reading of it is where the sound stopped being there for you.

What the sweep can do is find a sudden loss. A driver that fails at one frequency produces a hole — the tone vanishes and comes back — and that is very different from a gradual roll-off at the bottom or a fading top end. A hole is a fault. A fade is a characteristic.

And if you want the honest limit said plainly: this page sweeps 20 Hz to 1 kHz, not 20 kHz. The upper limit is a choice, not a limitation of the browser — a tone at 12 kHz is unpleasant to sit through and tells most people nothing they did not already know. If you want the top of the range, a dedicated measurement with a reference sweep played back and captured will do it properly, and that needs a microphone.

A sweep at the level you would use for music is genuinely unpleasant by the end. Start at about a third and raise it only if you need to.

A hole appears and disappears. A fade goes away and stays away. Only the first is a fault.

A difference between the ears at the bottom of the range is normal. A difference in the middle of the range is not.

Headphone settings that change your test results

Six switches. Each of them changes what you hear, and each of them is somewhere the fault is usually not. Read this list before you conclude that a pair is faulty.

The checks on the page are of the headphones and the operating system together, not of the headphones alone. A balance slider nudged one way makes one side quieter while every test still passes. Mono audio makes both channels identical while both still work perfectly. Neither is a fault in the hardware, and neither can be seen from the headphones alone.

About the paths below. The steps for Windows and macOS are written out in full, because those are the systems the guide was checked against. Phone makers each lay their settings out differently, and a fair number of phones have no balance control at all, so the phone rows below say what to look for rather than pretending to know where it is. If a path does not match what you see, search your own settings for the name of the setting itself.

The one-setting experiment. If something sounds wrong, change exactly one thing — the balance slider, or the mono audio switch — and run the check again. If the result is the same, the problem is not that setting, and you can stop looking at it.

Headphone test questions

The questions people actually ask about headphones, with the short answers. If yours is not here, the contact page is at the bottom of the site.

How do I test my headphones?

Put them on, turn the volume down to about a third, and open the Headset Check page. Play Left, then Right, then Both. The left tone should arrive in your left ear only, the right tone in your right ear only, and Both should sit in the middle of your head. Mark each side as you go and the page keeps your answers and tells you what that combination of marks means. It takes about ten seconds and nothing has to be installed.

How do I do a left and right audio test?

Play the left channel on its own and mark which ear you heard it in. Then do the same for the right. If each tone arrives in the opposite ear, your channels are crossed: the headphones are on the wrong way round, or a cable, adapter or splitter has the wiring reversed. If both tones sound identical in both ears, the signal is mono instead — a mono adapter, a three-pole plug in a four-pole socket, or mono audio switched on in the operating system. The page cannot tell those two apart for you, because it is a listening test and the answer is what you report back to it.

How do I know if my headphones are faulty?

Work through the list in this order, and stop when the fault disappears. Centre the balance slider. Push the plug fully home and look for lint in the socket. Confirm the headphones are the selected output device. Turn off mono audio, spatial sound and virtual surround. Flex the cable at the plug and at the split while a tone is playing. Then swap: another pair on this machine, and this pair on another machine. If the fault follows the headphones to a second machine, the pair is at fault. If it follows the machine to a second pair, the machine is. A fault that survives both swaps is a hardware fault, and the tone check tells you whether it is one driver failing at one frequency or the pair failing generally.

Why is one ear quieter than the other?

Check the left and right balance slider first. On Windows 11 it is in Settings → System → Sound → More sound settings → your device → Properties → Levels → Balance; on macOS it is in System Settings → Sound → Output. If the balance is centred and one side is still quieter, put the headphones on the other way round: if the quiet side follows the headphones, they are the cause; if it stays in the same ear, it is the fit or your hearing.

Why does only one side of my headphones play?

The balance slider, a plug that is not fully in, an output device that is not your headphones, or a broken cable. Work down that list in order — the slider is the most common and the least obvious of the four. If you have already swapped to another pair and the new one also plays on one side, the fault is in the device or its settings rather than in the headphones.

What does it mean if I hear the same sound in both ears?

It means the signal is mono. A mono adapter, a three-pole plug in a four-pole socket and a mono audio setting in the operating system will each do it. A mono signal cannot tell left from right, so every stereo cue — where a voice sits in a film, which side a car is coming from — collapses into the middle. Press Left in the channel check: if the tone sounds identical in both ears, that is what has happened.

How do I check whether one of my headphone drivers is wired backwards?

Use the polarity check in the advanced diagnostics. It plays a tone in both channels, then the same tone with one channel inverted, then the first version again, and asks which version was centred. With a correctly wired pair the first version sums and sits in the middle of your head and the second spreads out to both sides, because the two channels cancel. If the second version is the centred one, one driver is arriving with its phase flipped — a reversed driver still reaches the right ear, so the channel check cannot see it. If both versions sound the same, the signal is not reaching your headphones as stereo at all, which is a different fault: check that mono audio is off in your system settings.

Why does one ear sound thin, hollow or wider than the other?

Because the two drivers are not summing. One side is playing with its phase flipped, so it partly cancels the other instead of adding to it, and the sound spreads to the sides of your head rather than sitting in the middle. It is a wiring fault rather than a failed driver, so the tone still reaches the correct ear and the channel check passes. A repair, a replacement cable, or a plug pushed in upside down can leave a pair like this. The polarity check in the advanced diagnostics settles it by asking which of two versions was centred.

Can this test measure my headphone frequency response or sound quality?

No, and it does not pretend to. A browser can generate a tone; it cannot hear what comes back. The tone check gives you three fixed pitches to listen to, which is enough to spot a driver that has failed or a cable that is breaking up, and to hear the roll-off at the bottom of the range that most headphones have. Measuring a frequency response properly needs a reference signal played back and captured, and capturing it needs a microphone — a different kind of tool.

Why is a tone missing at one end of the range?

Two reasons, and both are ordinary. Headphones roll off at the bottom: the 100 Hz tone is the one most pairs play quietest, because the ear hears it less well and because most headphones give up their deepest bass first. And the roll-off is not even between sides, so a note that sounds full in one ear can sound hollow in the other if the two drivers are not matched. If a tone is missing rather than quiet, and the same happens on another device, then a driver has failed.

Does it work with earbuds and Bluetooth headphones?

Yes, with one caveat. The tone is generated in the browser and sent through whatever the operating system has chosen as the output, so Bluetooth is included — and a Bluetooth pair has faults of its own, such as an earbud not seated in the case or a device paired to two things at once. The caveat is that Bluetooth codecs compress the signal, so do not read the timbre of a wireless pair as a fault.

Why does my Bluetooth headset keep dropping one side?

Almost always the link rather than the headphones. An earbud that is not seated in the case, or not charged, will refuse to play while the other carries on, and a pair can end up half-paired to two devices at once. Put both buds back in the case, close the lid, open it again, and clean the charging contacts. Then forget the device on your phone and pair it again. If the headset keeps reconnecting on its own, something else — usually a computer — is holding it.

Why is there no sound at all?

The tone is generated in your browser, so if you can hear nothing at all, the sound never left the page. Check that the tab is not muted, that the system volume is up, and that the headphones are the selected output device rather than the built-in speakers. Browsers also throttle audio in a background tab, so keep the page in front of you. On a Bluetooth headset the connection itself is the usual culprit: remove the device from the Bluetooth menu and pair it again, and make sure it is not connected to something else at the same time.

Can this test my headset microphone?

No. This guide covers the headphone half of a headset. The microphone half can be measured rather than guessed at, because the browser can read the signal it asks for and report the processing it was given — but that is a separate test, and it is not here yet.

Do I need to install anything or give the page any permission?

No. Playing sound needs no permission, and there is nothing to install, no account and no download. The tones are generated in your browser with the Web Audio API, so nothing is fetched and nothing is uploaded. The page works offline once it has loaded.

What volume should I use for a headphone test?

About a third of your normal listening volume to begin with. A test tone carries far more energy than music at the same setting, because music spends most of its time at low levels and a tone does not. It is also continuous, so it is more tiring than music at the same volume. Start quiet, and raise it only once you know what you are listening to.

Can I use speakers instead of headphones?

You can, but the channel check stops meaning much. Sound from a speaker on your left reaches your right ear as well, so Left and Right will sound similar, and the test cannot tell a working pair from a mono one. Put headphones on for the channel check and the polarity check. The frequency sweep is fine on speakers.
