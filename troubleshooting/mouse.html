Mouse troubleshooting

What the numbers actually mean

Every reading the mouse test produces, what the threshold is, and what passing it actually tells you.

A raw measurement is only half the value. Here is what each result tells you in practice, and what to do about it. The thresholds below are the exact ones the Mouse Check page uses, so a verdict and this guide will always agree.

The click tester

The click tester

The fastest way to find out whether a mouse is actually broken.

Press every button in turn and watch the diagram: the left and right halves light up separately, the wheel lights when you press it as a button, and the two violet tabs on the left flank light when you press your thumb buttons. If one of them never lights, that button is the fault — and on a mouse with no thumb buttons, staying dark is the correct result, not a failure.

The scroll counter is the other one worth watching. A wheel that reports one notch for every physical detent is healthy. A wheel that reports two events for one notch, or none at all for some detents, has a worn encoder, and that is the first part of most mice to die. You can still use the wheel, but it will get worse.

One button never lights0 eventsThat button is the fault — hardware, not mapping

Two events for one notch2 events per detentWorn encoder — usually the first part to fail
Pointer precision

Pointer precision

Twenty targets, 26 pixels across, clicked one at a time.

Every target is 26 pixels across, small enough that hitting the centre takes deliberate aim but large enough that a healthy mouse on a normal surface manages it most of the time. Consistency matters more than the headline percentage: 60% on the first five targets followed by 95% on the next fifteen points to a surface or sensor problem, while a flat 80% throughout points to your sensitivity setting.

If accuracy is low: try a different surface, clean the sensor lens with a dry cloth, and check that the mouse is not being used at an extreme sensitivity setting. If accuracy is good on a mousepad and terrible on a desk, the desk is the problem, not the mouse.

ReadingThresholdWhat it means
95% or better≥ 95%“Your aim is very accurate.” Nothing to fix
85–94%≥ 85%“Your aim is accurate.” Normal for a mouse in use
70–84%≥ 70%“Your aim is a little loose.” Check the surface and the sensitivity setting
Below 70%< 70%“Your aim is loose” — the tool points you at your mouse sensitivity first

The tool also reports the average miss radius in pixels. On a good surface, a healthy mouse lands within roughly 8 px of the centre — the same threshold the drag test uses.

Double-click speed

Double-click speed

The interval between your two clicks, measured.

Your operating system treats two clicks as a double-click only if they land within a set window, 500 ms by default on Windows. A mouse with a worn or slow switch can push the second click past that boundary, and the result is maddening: you double-click a file and nothing opens, because the system saw two single clicks and the second one started a rename.

The fix is a setting, not a repair. Open your mouse settings and raise the double-click speed slider. If a comfortably fast double-click still fails to register, the switch itself is degraded and the mouse needs cleaning or replacing.

ReadingThresholdWhat it means
Under 300 ms< 300 ms“Snappy” — comfortably inside the 500 ms window, with headroom to spare
300–449 ms< 450 ms“Normal” — typical for a healthy mouse, but a quick hand movement can push it over
450 ms or more≥ 450 ms“Slow” — close to the 500 ms limit. Raise the double-click speed setting
Polling rate

Polling rate

How often your mouse reports its position — and whether that number can be trusted.

Polling rate is how often your mouse reports its position to the computer — 125 times a second on a basic office mouse, 500 on a mid-range gaming model, 1000 or more on a modern sensor. Below about 125 Hz the cursor feels like it is being dragged in steps. The figure matters most for competitive games, where the gap between a 125 Hz and a 1000 Hz mouse is the difference between a shot landing and not.

Read the confidence label. “Hardware-grade” means the browser exposed raw samples and the number is the mouse's real rate. “Estimated only” means the browser only reported standard move events, which are throttled to the display refresh — the number is a lower bound and should not be quoted as your mouse's rate.

ReadingThresholdWhat it means
1000 Hz or more≥ 1000 Hz“That is a pro-gaming mouse.” Hardware-grade
500–999 Hz≥ 500 Hz“That is a fast gaming mouse”
125–499 Hz≥ 125 Hz“That is a standard office mouse”
Below 125 Hz< 125 Hz“An unusually slow polling rate” — the cursor will feel stepped

The advanced measurements

The advanced measurements

Four more readings, for when the basics all pass and something still feels wrong.

Drag accuracy measures how far your pointer lands from the centre of a target when you are dragging rather than clicking — and that is where an odd sensitivity curve shows up first.

Scroll encoder counts notches in each direction and reports the pixel delta per notch. A delta that varies wildly between notches is the classic encoder warning sign.

Motion smoothness reports the mean gap between pointer move events and how much that gap varies. The mean is mostly a property of your display rather than your mouse, because the browser delivers pointer events once per frame — see the display refresh rate section below for what that means in practice. The jitter is the half that does belong to the mouse: wireless mice add variability whenever the 2.4 GHz link renegotiates with the receiver, and a large jitter figure often disappears when you switch to the cable or move the receiver to a USB port on the same side of the desk.

Hover response measures how long the browser takes to get the hover to the page — the gap between the platform creating the event and the page receiving it. A delay of 60 ms or more makes menus feel sluggish, and it is frequently the display’s frame time rather than anything the mouse did. Browsers deliver pointer events once per frame, so the delay is bounded by a single frame period — 16.7 ms on a 60 Hz display, 6.9 ms on a 144 Hz one.

Why is my mouse not double-clicking?

Usually a double-click window that is too tight, occasionally a worn switch. The guide has the fix for each, and a test that measures your actual interval.

The test that settles it

The double-click test measures the actual interval between your two clicks and tells you how much headroom you have before the 500 ms window closes. Run the double-click test

The most common cause is not a broken mouse at all. Your operating system has a double-click window — 500 ms on Windows by default — and both clicks have to land inside it. If you are moving your hand between clicks, the second one arrives after the window has closed and the system logs two separate single clicks instead. The result is a file that opens and immediately drops into rename mode, or a link that never opens at all.

The fix is a setting rather than a repair: raise the double-click speed slider in your mouse settings. The double-click speed test measures the actual interval between your two clicks, so you can see how much headroom you have before it becomes a problem.

How to read it

What your interval means

The tool measures the gap between your two clicks and compares it against the 500 ms window the system uses.

What to try

In order

Measure the interval

Run the double-click test. If it says “Normal” or better, timing is not your problem and you can skip to step 4.

Raise the double-click speed

On Windows: Settings → Bluetooth & devices → Mouse → Related settings → Additional mouse settings → the Buttons tab → the Speed slider.

Re-measure

Run the test again. If the interval is unchanged but the behaviour is fixed, the setting was the problem.

Suspect the switch

If a deliberately fast double-click still registers as two single clicks, the switch is degraded. Clean it, or replace the mouse.

Why does my mouse click on its own?

A switch that is making contact at rest, or a wireless link reporting a stale button state. The click tester tells you which.

The test that settles it

The click tester counts every press it receives. Leave the mouse alone and watch which counter moves — that is what tells a worn switch from injected clicks. Run the click tester

A mouse registering clicks you did not make has a problem in one of three places. A worn or dirty switch under the left button can make contact twice per press, or worse, make contact while the button sits at rest. A wireless mouse that has lost its link can report a stale button state when it reconnects. Or it is software — an automation tool, a remote desktop session or a screen recorder injecting clicks of its own.

The click tester settles it: leave the mouse alone and watch the counters. If the left-click count climbs without you touching anything, the fault is in the hardware or the wireless link. If it stays still, something on the machine is generating the clicks.

How to read it

What the counter means

The click tester counts every press it receives, including the ones you did not make.

The click tester counts presses, including phantom ones. The number tells you which of the three causes you have.

ReadingThresholdWhat it means
Counter stays at zero while you touch nothing0 eventsThe hardware and the link are clean — something on the machine is injecting clicks
Left-click count climbs on its ownevents without inputA worn switch or a stale wireless button state. Clean the switch, or re-pair the receiver
One button alone misbehavesisolated faultThat switch, not the mouse. A mouse that misbehaves on every button is a link or driver problem
What to try

In order

Watch the counter

Leave the mouse alone for a minute with the click tester open. Note which counter moves, if any.

Rule out software

Close screen recorders, automation tools and remote desktop sessions, then watch again. A macro utility can register clicks that never happened.

Rule out the link

For a wireless mouse, re-seat the receiver or re-pair the device. A link that drops and reconnects can replay the last button state.

Clean the switch

Compressed air under the button, then a drop of isopropyl alcohol around the stem. Chattering that survives cleaning is a worn switch.

Why is my mouse unresponsive or not moving?

Asleep, on the wrong input, driver problem, or dead. Four faults that all look identical until you test them.

The test that settles it

Whether the pointer moves and whether the buttons register is the whole diagnosis. The click tester shows both. Run the click tester

“Unresponsive mouse” covers four separate faults, and the difference between them decides what you do next. The mouse is asleep, which happens when a wireless mouse has idled long enough to power down and the first click is consumed waking it up. It is on the wrong input, which happens when a wireless receiver is plugged into another machine or a Bluetooth device is paired to the wrong computer. It is a driver problem, where the operating system has stopped updating the device and a reconnect fixes it. Or it is genuinely dead.

The mouse test above tells you which: if not one button registers but the pointer still moves, the hardware is fine and the problem is upstream in the connection or the driver. If nothing at all happens, the mouse itself has failed.

How to read it

What the click tester means

Two observations separate four faults: does the pointer move, and do the buttons register?

Two observations — movement and button events — split the four causes apart.

What to try

In order

Move it and click

If the first press after a pause does nothing, the mouse was asleep. That is normal for a wireless mouse and is not a fault.

Check the input

Wireless receivers are the classic cause. Confirm the receiver is plugged into this machine, and that a Bluetooth mouse is paired to this computer and not a neighbour's.

Reconnect the driver

Unplug and replug, or turn the device off and on. On Windows, Device Manager → Mice and other pointing devices → disable and re-enable the device.

Test elsewhere

Plug into another port, or try the mouse on another machine. If it is dead in both, the mouse is dead.

Why is my scroll wheel skipping or jittering?

A worn scroll encoder, usually the first part of a mouse to fail. How to clean it, and how to tell it apart from a bad surface.

The test that settles it

The scroll encoder test counts notches in each direction and reports the pixel delta per notch — the number that tells a worn encoder from a bad surface. Run the encoder test

A wheel that moves in uneven steps, or jumps two notches at a time, is almost always the scroll encoder wearing out. The encoder is a small wheel with contacts that a spring pushes against as the scroll wheel turns, and dust, dried lubricant and a tired spring all make it skip or double-fire. It is the first part of most mice to fail, usually months before the buttons go.

Cleaning the wheel with a burst of compressed air and a drop of isopropyl alcohol on the axle can rescue a lightly fouled encoder. Beyond that the encoder needs replacing, which in practice means the mouse does. The scroll encoder test in the advanced section counts notches in each direction and reports the pixel delta per notch, which is how you tell a worn encoder from a surface that is simply too slippery.

How to read it

What the encoder test means

The test counts notches in each direction and reports the pixel delta per notch. A healthy wheel is consistent.

What to try

In order

Measure the notches

Open the advanced diagnostics and scroll up and down a fixed number of times. Note whether the count matches and whether the pixel delta stays steady.

Blow it out

Compressed air under the wheel while it spins. Most encoder faults are dust, and this fixes a surprising share of them.

Clean the axle

A drop of isopropyl alcohol on the wheel axle, then spin it by hand to work it in.

Decide

If the delta still varies, the encoder is worn through. That is a replacement, and in practice that means a new mouse.

Why does my cursor jump or freeze randomly?

A dirty sensor throwing the pointer, or a wireless link renegotiating. The pattern of the jump tells you which one it is.

The test that narrows it down

Neither reading detects a jump outright. What they do is separate the two causes: the pointer precision test tells you whether the sensor is throwing the pointer or the surface is, and the motion test tells you whether the link is dropping packets. Run the motion test

The pointer teleporting across the screen without you moving the mouse has two classic causes. The first is a dirty or damaged sensor: a speck of dust or a hair under the mouse gets picked up and thrown, so the sensor reports a sudden change in position and the cursor jumps. The second is wireless interference, where the link renegotiates with the receiver and the pointer snaps back to the last known position.

Move the mouse in slow circles and watch for a pattern. Jumps that correlate with fast, jerky movement point at the surface. Jumps that arrive at a steady rate on a clean mousepad point at the radio — move the receiver to a USB port on the same side of the desk as the mouse and see whether it stops. No reading here sees the jump itself, so treat the motion and precision results as the rule-out rather than the proof.

How to read it

What the motion reading means

The motion reading is the mean gap between pointer events and how much that gap varies. The jitter half is where a link problem shows. The precision reading in the last row comes from the pointer precision test.

What to try

In order

Clean the sensor

A dry cloth on the lens, and compressed air under the mouse. A single hair under the sensor is enough to throw the pointer.

Change the surface

Test on a mousepad and on a desk. If the jumps only happen on one of them, the surface is the cause.

Move the receiver

Put the receiver in a USB port on the same side of the desk as the mouse, not behind the monitor or in a hub.

Use the cable

If the jumps stop on the wired connection, it is the radio. Charge the battery and check the 2.4 GHz interference from nearby wireless gear.

Why are my mouse side buttons not working?

Almost never broken hardware. The buttons fire fine; the operating system just has no instruction for what to do with them.

The test that settles it

Press each thumb button and watch the violet tabs on the diagram. If they light, the hardware is fine and you need mapping software. Run the click tester

Side buttons are almost never handled by the operating system directly. They are reported as extra mouse buttons, and mapping them to something useful — browser back and forward being the common default — needs either the manufacturer's configuration software or a generic remapping utility. On a machine with no such software, the buttons still physically fire but the browser has no instruction for what to do with them.

That distinction is exactly what the click tester makes visible. If the violet thumb tabs light up when you press them, the hardware is fine and you need software to bind them. If they stay dark, the problem is the button or the wireless pairing, not the mapping.

One browser exception is worth knowing. Firefox uses the back and forward mouse buttons for history navigation and never passes them to the page, so the tabs stay dark there even when the hardware is fine. Chromium-based browsers do deliver them, as button 3 and button 4.

How to read it

What the click tester means

The violet tabs on the left flank of the diagram light when the side buttons fire. That single observation splits the fault in two.

One observation — do the tabs light? — separates hardware from mapping.

ReadingThresholdWhat it means
The violet tabs light upevents receivedThe hardware works. The buttons simply have no instruction — install mapping software

They light, but do nothing usefulevents received, no actionThe browser has them bound to nothing. Remap them in the browser or in software
What to try

In order

Check the diagram

Press each thumb button in turn and watch the violet tabs. This tells you whether you have a mapping problem or a hardware one.

Check the browser

Middle and side buttons are remappable in the browser. Look for a mouse-button extension, and check that nothing has bound them to something else.

Install the maker's software

Logitech, Razer, SteelSeries and the rest all ship configuration software that binds the extra buttons. Without it, the buttons fire and nothing happens.

Check the driver

Device Manager → Mice and other pointing devices. A missing or stale driver stops the extra buttons being reported at all.

Mouse settings that change your test results

The Windows and macOS switches that move your numbers — and how to use one as a controlled experiment.

The same mouse, in the same hand, on two different machines, can produce two different sets of numbers. Almost all of that difference comes from four settings — and none of them are on the mouse.

This guide is the one to read when a result looks wrong and you are not sure whether the mouse is at fault. Change one setting, run the test again, and compare. That turns a vague feeling into an experiment.

Pointer speed

Pointer speed — the setting that changes every accuracy score

On Windows 11 the pointer speed slider lives under Settings → Bluetooth & devices → Mouse, and the same page carries the “Enhance pointer precision” toggle. On Windows 10 the equivalent is Settings → Devices → Mouse, and the two classic options are in the legacy dialog under Additional mouse options → Pointer Options tab — the pointer speed slider is under Motion, and “Enhance pointer precision” is the checkbox beneath it.

On macOS the equivalent is System Settings → Mouse → Point & Click → “Tracking speed”, and the acceleration curve is under Advanced → “Pointer acceleration”.

Here is why this matters more than it looks. Pointer speed scales how far the pointer travels for a given physical movement. A high setting means your hand travels less to cross the screen, and the pointer precision test — which asks you to hit a 26 px target — becomes correspondingly harder. Turn the setting down and the accuracy figure rises without the mouse changing at all.

The practical consequence: an accuracy score is meaningless on its own. It is a score for a mouse at a given pointer speed. If you want to compare two mice, set both pointer speeds to the same number first. If you want to know why your accuracy dropped since last week, check the setting before you blame the mouse.

Pointer acceleration

Pointer acceleration — the setting that makes aiming feel wrong

Acceleration means the pointer does not move at a fixed ratio to your hand. It moves further when you move fast and more precisely when you move slowly. On Windows this is “Enhance pointer precision”, on by default. On macOS it is “Pointer acceleration”, also on by default, and Apple's own documentation puts it plainly: by default the pointer moves more quickly for fast movements and more precisely for slow ones.

Acceleration is the reason the pointer precision test can behave like it is punishing you for aiming carefully. You settle on a target, slow down to place the click, and the sensitivity curve hands you a slower response exactly when you have switched to fine control. That is the moment of maximum error, and the curve makes it worse.

It is also the reason the drag test can fail while the click test passes. Dragging is a sustained, slow movement — exactly the regime where the acceleration curve changes most.

The experiment

Run the pointer precision test and note the percentage. Turn “Enhance pointer precision” off (Windows) or “Pointer acceleration” off (macOS), and run it again. If the second number is materially higher, you had been fighting a curve rather than a mouse fault. The drag accuracy test is the sharper signal here: the tool treats a drag offset of under 8 px as “as accurate while dragging as while clicking”, so any drag gap that closes when acceleration is off points at the curve.

Double-click speed

Double-click speed — where Windows and macOS disagree

On Windows the slider is tucked one level deeper than you would expect: Settings → Bluetooth & devices → Mouse → Related settings → Additional mouse settings, then the Buttons tab, then the Speed slider under Double-click speed. The legacy route is Control Panel → Mouse → Buttons tab. The default window is 500 ms.

On macOS there is no millisecond window at all. The setting is System Settings → Accessibility → Pointer Control → “Double-click speed”, and it offers Slow, Medium and Fast rather than a slider.

This is worth knowing if you have moved between the two, because a mouse tuned on one platform routinely feels broken on the other. A muscle memory learned against a 500 ms window is much slower than macOS's Medium setting expects, and the first double-click on a new machine lands as two single clicks.

Display refresh rate

Display refresh rate — the setting that is not about the mouse at all

Hover response measures how long the browser takes to get the hover to the page. The tool calls anything under 25 ms “instantly” and anything at 60 ms or more slow. That delay is usually the display’s own frame time rather than anything the mouse did.

So if hover feels sluggish but every other reading is fine, change the monitor’s refresh rate before you open the mouse. A 144 Hz display reports a shorter hover delay than a 60 Hz one for an identical mouse, because the display answers sooner — not because the sensor got any faster.

The same effect shows up in motion smoothness, and it is worth knowing before you read that result. Because the browser delivers pointer events once per frame, the average gap between them is roughly the frame period: 16.7 ms on a 60 Hz display, 8.3 ms on a 120 Hz one. A perfect mouse on a 60 Hz display therefore reads “Fair” rather than “Smooth”. That is the display, not the mouse, and a faster display makes the number smaller rather than larger.

Put it together

The one-setting experiment

If a reading looks wrong, change one setting and re-run. Do not change two, or you will not know which one did it.

Record the baseline

Run the pointer precision test and the drag accuracy test. Write down both numbers and the pointer speed setting they were taken at.

Change one thing

Start with pointer acceleration, because it is the switch most often left on without anyone knowing it was there.

Re-run, same conditions

Same mouse, same pad, same surface, same twenty targets. The drag test is the sharper of the two, because it is the one that moves when the curve changes.

Decide what you have

If the numbers moved, the setting was the cause and the mouse is fine. If they did not move, the mouse or its surface is the problem — and the symptom sections above cover that.
