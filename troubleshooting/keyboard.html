What the numbers actually mean

This is the fastest way to find out whether a keyboard is actually broken. Press every key and watch the map. Three things to look for. A key that never lights up is either disconnected, obstructed by debris, or being read by a different input device. A key that stays lit after you have let go is mechanically stuck — a very common symptom of spilled liquid. And a key that fires twice for one physical press is chattering, which usually means a worn switch that will eventually stop registering the key at all.

Function keys are included for reference, but many browsers and operating systems reserve them, so they will not light up. That is not a fault in your keyboard.

The words-per-minute figure is calculated from correct characters only, and accuracy is reported next to it because a fast 90% accurate typist is producing errors that slow them down in real work. There is no autocorrect, no dictionary and no word completion, which is exactly why this number usually sits below the one your operating system's built-in test reports.

The accuracy figure is the diagnostically interesting one. Above 97% means the switches are clean. A persistent error on one specific character, with the rest of the text perfect, is nearly always a single worn or obstructed key rather than a typing problem. Errors spread evenly across the whole text point somewhere else, usually to a hand position or a bad habit rather than to hardware.

Browsers report both the key you pressed and where it physically is. The second value is a property of the hardware and never changes, while the first depends on which layout the operating system has selected. Comparing the two against known layouts tells you what your board actually is.

This is worth running once on any second-hand or refurbished machine, because a mismatched layout is responsible for an enormous amount of low-grade frustration: typing ; produces ö, quotation marks come out in the wrong place, and shortcuts like Ctrl+Z behave inconsistently. The fix is usually a single setting in the system's keyboard preferences.

Cheap membrane keyboards are usually limited to two keys at once. Press three and the third is ignored, or worse, the first two produce the wrong characters because the controller cannot tell which circuit closed. Gaming keyboards add anti-ghosting hardware and typically handle six or more simultaneously.

You will feel this long before you read about it. In a game, a three-key combination — sprint, jump and reload — silently drops the third. In a shortcut like Ctrl+Shift+Esc, the escape key is the one that never arrives. If that is happening, the rollover test will confirm it immediately.

This measures the gap between the browser registering your keypress and the next moment the page is able to draw a frame. Because the clock starts on the input event rather than on the screen changing, the number reflects input handling and your display's refresh rate rather than how quickly you can react — you cannot press faster than your own reflexes, so a reaction-timed test would only ever measure you.

A healthy setup lands somewhere inside a single frame interval: under about 9 ms on a 120 Hz display, under 17 ms on a 60 Hz one. The panel measures your actual refresh rate and shows it next to the result, so you can see how much of the figure is the display rather than the keyboard.

Jitter is the more useful half of this result. A keyboard with a 12 ms average and a 2 ms jitter feels precise. One with a 9 ms average and a 25 ms jitter feels unpredictable, because input arrives in bursts. High jitter on a wired keyboard usually points to polling interference, a busy system, or a controller that is struggling.

Why is my keyboard not typing?

If nothing types at all, the first thing to establish is whether the computer can see the keyboard at all. The key tester answers that in one second: press a key and see whether a cap lights up — and whether it lights up tells you which half of the stack to look at next.

If caps light up, the keyboard is connected and the fault is in the application — a stuck modifier key, a text field that will not accept focus, or a shortcut that has captured the key. If nothing lights up, the fault is below the application layer. A wireless keyboard may simply be asleep or unpaired, which is fixed by switching it on or re-pairing it. A laptop keyboard that has gone completely dead is usually a hardware failure of the ribbon cable or the controller board, which is a repair job rather than a setting.

Check the key tester

Press a handful of keys. If caps light up, the keyboard is connected and the problem is in the application.

Look for a stuck modifier

A held Ctrl, Shift or Alt swallows everything else. Press and release each modifier on its own and watch the lamp row.

Close the focused field and retry

Some text fields lose focus without losing the keyboard. Click somewhere else and type again.

Rule out the wireless link

If it is Bluetooth, switch it off and on, or re-pair it. A live USB keyboard in the same port tells you which side is broken.

Treat a dead laptop keyboard as a repair

A ribbon cable or controller board failure is not a setting. It is a repair or a replacement.

Why are some keys on my keyboard not working?

Individual dead keys are almost always physical. A switch that has been flooded with liquid and then dried out stops making contact, and so does a switch worn out by a decade of the same key being hammered — the Enter key and the space bar are the usual casualties because they take the most use.

A key that works intermittently is a different problem. That points to debris under the keycap, or a marginal contact that only closes when the key is pressed slightly off-centre. The key tester separates the two cases for you: a key that never registers and a key that registers unreliably look the same on the keyboard itself, and the event log makes the difference obvious.

The test that settles it

The event log shows whether a key never registered at all, or registered on some presses and not others. Run the key tester

The key never appears in the log0 eventsA dead switch — liquid damage, or a switch worn out by a decade of the same key
The key appears only on some pressesintermittentDebris under the keycap, or a marginal contact that only closes slightly off-centre

Find the dead key on the map

Press every key in turn and note which ones never light up. More than one is unusual and points elsewhere.

Pull the keycap off

Most intermittent faults are a crumb or a piece of grit under the cap. A keycap puller or a gentle nudge off a flat surface gets it off.

Clean the switch

Compressed air under the cap, and a drop of isopropyl alcohol if the switch has ever been wet. Work it in by pressing the key repeatedly.

Check the liquid history

A keyboard that has had a drink fails weeks later, not immediately. If there was a spill, suspect the switches around it.

Replace the switch if it stays dead

A hot-swap board lets you pull the switch and put a new one in. A soldered board means a repair shop.

Why does my keyboard type the wrong character?

This is nearly always a layout mismatch rather than a broken keyboard. Your keyboard has a physical layout that never changes — the key labelled Z is always in the same position — while your operating system has a layout setting that maps that position to a character. If the system believes it is German but the board is British, the key in that position produces a different symbol and you cannot type an apostrophe or a semicolon properly.

The physical layout test reads the position of every key you press against the known layouts, so you can compare what the hardware is with what the system has selected and correct the mismatch in the keyboard preferences.

Run the layout test

Type a sentence in your normal layout. The analyser reports which physical board you have and how confident it is.

Compare with the system setting

On Windows, check Settings → Time & language → Language & region → the keyboard options. On macOS, check System Settings → Keyboard → Input Sources.

Switch the layout to match the hardware

The physical key labelled Z stays where it is; the system decides what it means. Change the system setting, not the keycap.

Check the modifier state

A stuck Alt or Ctrl changes what a key produces. Press and release each modifier alone before changing layouts.

Re-run the test

The layout reading should now agree with what the keyboard is producing.

Why is my keyboard ghosting?

Ghosting is what happens when the keyboard cannot tell which of several simultaneously pressed keys closed its circuit, so it registers the wrong combination or drops one entirely. Budget membrane keyboards usually handle two keys at once and no more, which means a three-key combination like Ctrl+Shift+Esc delivers the wrong two of the three.

Gaming keyboards solve this with anti-ghosting hardware, typically handling six or more keys at once. The rollover test holds four keys at once and reports how many the keyboard actually delivered, so you know whether you are looking at a two-key board or a proper one.

The test that settles it

The rollover test holds four keys at once and reports how many the keyboard actually delivered, so you know whether you are looking at a two-key board or a proper one. Run the rollover test

ReadingThresholdWhat it means
4 keys register4 / 4Full rollover — nothing is being dropped
3 keys register3 / 4Meets the bar most gaming keyboards clear. Acceptable for almost everything
2 keys register2 / 4A two-key board. The third key in a shortcut is silently dropped

Run the rollover test

Hold the keys the test asks for and see how many the board actually delivers.

Reproduce the real shortcut

Try the combination you actually use — Ctrl+Shift+Esc, or a three-key game bind — and watch which one never arrives.

Check the modifier order

Some cheap boards resolve modifiers in a fixed order, so Ctrl+Shift+Esc delivers Ctrl+Esc and drops Shift.

Test on another machine

If the same board rolls over fine elsewhere, the fault is the machine, not the keyboard.

Accept it, or change the keyboard

There is no setting that adds rollover hardware. If a shortcut matters, you need a board with anti-ghosting.

Why does my keyboard feel laggy?

Two different things get called laggy, and they need different fixes. The first is input delay — the gap between the keypress reaching the computer and the screen responding — which is usually the display's frame rate rather than the keyboard. A 60 Hz monitor adds up to 17 ms to everything, which is often enough to notice in a game.

The second is key chatter, where a worn switch fires twice for one press and the duplicate character appears a moment later, which reads as the computer being slow. The response latency test measures the first of the two and separates it from reaction time; the key tester shows you the second by flagging any key that registered twice from one press.

Separate delay from chatter

Run the latency test for the delay, and the key tester for chatter. They are different faults with different fixes.

Check the display refresh rate

A 60 Hz monitor adds up to 17 ms to everything. A 120 Hz or 144 Hz display removes most of what feels like keyboard lag.

Look at the measured refresh rate

The panel reports your actual display refresh next to the latency figure, so you can see how much of the number is the screen.

Close what is running

A busy CPU delays the frame the input is waiting for. Quit heavy apps and re-measure.

Test on a wired connection

Wireless adds a variable hop to every keystroke. A wired board that is smooth on the same machine proves the link was the problem.

Why is one key stuck down?

A key that stays pressed when you have released it is mechanically stuck. The usual causes are a swollen keycap, something sticky that has leaked under the key, or a switch that has failed closed. The symptom is usually worse than it looks: because the operating system believes the key is being held, the character repeats continuously until you can clear it.

If the key is merely sticky, pulling off the keycap and cleaning underneath usually fixes it. If the switch itself has failed closed, the keyboard needs a switch replacement or a new keyboard — and if the stuck key is the space bar, a common cause is a switch that has flooded and then corroded.

The test that settles it

The key map shows a key that is still lit after you have let go, which is the whole diagnosis in one glance. Run the key tester

Confirm it is the key and not the software

If the cap stays lit on the map, the key is not reporting a release, and no amount of reinstalling will change that.

Clear the repeat

Press and release the key. If the character stops repeating, the system is fine and the switch is the problem.

Pull the keycap off

Look underneath for a sticky drink, a sweet, or a fragment of a broken switch. This fixes more stuck keys than anything else.

Clean and work the switch

Compressed air, a drop of isopropyl alcohol, then press the key repeatedly to work the fluid in.

Replace the switch if it stays stuck

A failed-closed switch will not come back on its own. Hot-swap boards make this a two-minute job.

The trackpad is a different device

On a laptop the trackpad is the same slab of glass as the keyboard, which is why the trackpad is tested on the Keyboard Check page. But it is not the same kind of device, and it does not have the same faults.

A mouse has a sensor that can get dirty and a wheel that can wear out. A trackpad has neither. It has a pressure sensor that reads the shape of a contact, a firmware layer that turns that shape into movement and gestures, and an operating system that applies its own acceleration and filtering on top. That is why its problems are so often fixed by a setting rather than by a repair, and why a right-click that never arrives usually means a gesture is switched off rather than that the hardware is broken.

There is also a number that reads wrong if you know what a mouse reports. A trackpad commonly reports between 60 and 120 times a second, because it is calculating where your finger is. A mouse is polled electrically at 500 or 1000 times a second and reports every one of those. The same figure that would look alarmingly slow on a mouse is the ordinary range for a trackpad.

All of that has its own long guide, because none of it belongs to the keyboard: the trackpad troubleshooting guide.
