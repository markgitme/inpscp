What the mouse test numbers actually mean

A raw measurement is only half the value. Here is each reading, the threshold the tool applies to it, and what a pass or a fail actually tells you about your mouse.

Written by Mark · Updated 28 October 2026

A raw measurement is only half the value. Here is what each result tells you in practice, and what to do about it. The thresholds below are the exact ones the Mouse Check page uses, so a verdict and this guide will always agree.

The click tester

The click tester

The fastest way to find out whether a mouse is actually broken.

Press every button in turn and watch the diagram: the left and right halves light up separately, the wheel lights when you press it as a button, and the two violet tabs on the left flank light when you press your thumb buttons. If one of them never lights, that button is the fault — and on a mouse with no thumb buttons, staying dark is the correct result, not a failure.

The scroll counter is the other one worth watching. A wheel that reports one notch for every physical detent is healthy. A wheel that reports two events for one notch, or none at all for some detents, has a worn encoder, and that is the first part of most mice to die. You can still use the wheel, but it will get worse.

Pointer precision

Pointer precision

Twenty targets, 26 pixels across, clicked one at a time.

Every target is 26 pixels across, small enough that hitting the centre takes deliberate aim but large enough that a healthy mouse on a normal surface manages it most of the time. Consistency matters more than the headline percentage: 60% on the first five targets followed by 95% on the next fifteen points to a surface or sensor problem, while a flat 80% throughout points to your sensitivity setting.

If accuracy is low: try a different surface, clean the sensor lens with a dry cloth, and check that the mouse is not being used at an extreme sensitivity. If accuracy is good on a mousepad and terrible on a desk, the desk is the problem, not the mouse.

ReadingThresholdWhat it means
95% or better≥ 95%“Your aim is very accurate.” Nothing to fix
85–94%≥ 85%“Your aim is accurate.” Normal for a mouse in use
70–84%≥ 70%“Your aim is a little loose.” Check the surface and the sensitivity setting
Below 70%< 70%“Your aim is loose” — the tool points you at your mouse sensitivity first

The tool also reports the average miss radius in pixels. On a good surface, a healthy mouse lands within roughly 8 px of the centre — the same threshold the drag test uses.

Double-click speed

Double-click speed

The interval between your two clicks, measured.

Your operating system treats two clicks as a double-click only if they land within a set window, 500 ms by default on Windows. A mouse with a worn or slow switch can push the second click past that boundary, and the result is maddening: you double-click a file and nothing opens, because the system saw two single clicks and the second one started a rename.

The fix is a setting, not a repair. Open your mouse settings and raise the double-click speed slider. If a comfortably fast double-click still fails to register, the switch itself is degraded and the mouse needs cleaning or replacing.

ReadingThresholdWhat it means
Under 300 ms< 300 ms“Snappy” — comfortably inside the 500 ms window
300–449 ms< 450 ms“Normal” — typical for a healthy mouse, but the margin is thinner than you think
450 ms or more≥ 450 ms“Slow” — close to the 500 ms limit. Raise the double-click speed setting
Polling rate

Polling rate

How often your mouse reports its position — and whether that number can be trusted.

Polling rate is how often your mouse reports its position to the computer — 125 times a second on a basic office mouse, 500 on a mid-range gaming model, 1000 or more on a modern sensor. Below about 125 Hz the cursor feels like it is being dragged in steps. The figure matters most for competitive games, where the gap between a 125 Hz and a 1000 Hz mouse is the difference between a shot landing and not.

Read the confidence label. “Hardware-grade” means the browser exposed raw samples and the number is the mouse's real rate. “Estimated only” means the browser only reported standard move events, which are throttled to the display refresh — the number is a lower bound and should not be quoted as your mouse's rate.

ReadingThresholdWhat it means
1000 Hz or more≥ 1000 Hz“That is a pro-gaming mouse.” Hardware-grade
500–999 Hz≥ 500 Hz“That is a fast gaming mouse”
125–499 Hz≥ 125 Hz“That is a standard office mouse”
Below 125 Hz< 125 Hz“An unusually slow polling rate” — the cursor will feel stepped

The advanced measurements

The advanced measurements

Four more readings, for when the basics all pass and something still feels wrong.

Drag accuracy measures how far your pointer lands from the centre of a target when you are dragging rather than clicking, which is where an odd sensitivity curve shows up first.

Scroll encoder counts notches in each direction and reports the pixel delta per notch. A delta that varies wildly between notches is the classic encoder warning sign.

Motion smoothness reports the mean gap between pointer move events and how much that gap varies. Wireless mice add variability whenever the 2.4 GHz link renegotiates with the receiver, and a large jitter figure often disappears when you switch to the cable or move the receiver to a USB port on the same side of the desk.

Hover response measures the delay between the pointer entering a target and the first movement event being delivered. A delay of 60 ms or more makes menus feel sluggish, and it is frequently caused by a high display poll rate rather than by the mouse.
